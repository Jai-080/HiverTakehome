# AmazonHelp Support Triage — Report

## 1. Problem framing

**What "good" means here.** AmazonHelp is the largest single-brand slice of the Customer Support on Twitter dataset (169,840 replies vs. AppleSupport's 106,860, out of 2,811,774 total rows) — high enough volume that triage automation is worth building, and messy enough (heavy DM-deflection, PII in raw text, sarcasm, fraud claims, vocabulary overlap between intents) that a naive approach fails in instructive ways. "Good" for this system means three things, in order of importance: (1) never auto-send a reply on a message that needed a human — the escalation policy is deliberately conservative, trading precision for recall on that axis; (2) ground replies in the brand's actual historical resolution pattern rather than invented specifics — no fabricated order numbers, ship dates, or refund amounts; (3) beat a naive classifier and a "do nothing clever" baseline on intent and escalation, which it does (Section 2).

**What was deliberately not built:**
- **No Banking77.** Considered and excluded — its intents (card disputes, exchange rates, international transfers) don't map onto AmazonHelp's actual traffic (delivery, refunds, account access, marketplace trust). Using it would have been scope confusion dressed up as rigor, not a real head start.
- **No cloud API dependency in the final pipeline.** The original plan used Gemini for embeddings, classification, drafting, and judging. Two free-tier quota walls forced a full pivot to a local stack: `embed_content` capped at 1,000 requests/day (exhausted after 1,001 calls across two runs), and `generate_content` capped at 20 requests/day / 5/minute on this project — confirmed via the AI Studio dashboard, not assumed, before committing further budget to it. The system now runs entirely on local models: `BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5` for all embeddings (clustering, RAG retrieval, query encoding) and `microsoft/Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct` (4-bit quantized via `bitsandbytes`, GPU-accelerated on an RTX 3050 after discovering the installed `torch` was silently a CPU-only build) for classification, reply drafting, and LLM-judging. This is a deliberate scope change, not an oversight — it also means the system has zero per-request cloud cost or rate-limit ceiling once running.

## 2. Results vs. baselines

Evaluated on the 200-row hand-labeled golden set (`reports/golden_set_candidates_labeled.csv`) against three systems: a trivial baseline (majority-class intent + always-escalate), a simple baseline (TF-IDF + Logistic Regression, intent-only), and the full system.

| Method | Intent Accuracy | Intent Macro-F1 | Escalation Precision | Escalation Recall | Escalation F1 | Escalation Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trivial baseline | 0.240 | 0.048 | 0.345 | 1.000 | 0.513 | 0.345 |
| Simple baseline | 0.380 | 0.165 | 1.000 | 0.043 | 0.083 | 0.670 |
| Full system | 0.645 | 0.579 | 0.411 | 0.870 | 0.558 | 0.525 |

Stated plainly: **the full system wins on both metrics that matter** — intent macro-F1 (0.579 vs. 0.165 and 0.048) and escalation F1 (0.558 vs. 0.083 and 0.513) — by a wide margin over both baselines. That win is not free: escalation precision is the lowest of the three methods (0.411), because the policy is deliberately biased toward escalating rather than auto-handling on uncertain ground. The full system's escalation confusion matrix makes the tradeoff concrete:

|  | Predicted auto | Predicted escalate |
|---|---|---|
| **True auto** (n=131) | 45 | 86 |
| **True escalate** (n=69) | 9 | 60 |

86 of 131 true-auto messages get escalated anyway (the precision cost), but only 9 of 69 true-escalate messages slip through as auto-handled (the recall win, 0.870). Given that an auto-handled message goes out with no human review, this is the correct side of the tradeoff to be on for a support system — but it means roughly two-thirds of true-auto traffic still reaches a human today, which is a real operating cost, not a rounding error.

## 3. Failure analysis

Five failure modes, pulled from `surface_failure_candidates.py`'s output against the golden set. Full detail in `reports/failure_analysis.md`; summary here:

1. **Refund/return language systematically misread as delivery or billing** (fixable). Every one of the top 5 intent misclassifications is the same confusion pair — `refund_return_dispute` mistaken for `delivery_shipping_issue` or `billing_subscription_dispute` — every one at `predicted_confidence=0.95`. The classifier is pattern-matching payment/delivery vocabulary instead of the actual ask (money back). The 0.95 confidence on every wrong call is itself evidence the confidence score isn't well-calibrated to correctness.
2. **That confusion cascades into missed escalations** (fixable only by fixing #1). `refund_return_dispute` is a high-risk always-escalate intent; three of the nine missed escalations are misclassification cases from #1 that never reached the escalation gate because they were never labeled that intent. The escalation policy logic is sound in isolation — the failure is entirely upstream, a concrete example of a safety gate being only as strong as the classifier feeding it.
3. **Retrieval grounds tone as well as content, and gets it backwards on bad news** (partially fixable, currently a fundamental limitation of pure similarity retrieval). One customer reporting a defective product got a reply reading "We're thrilled to hear about your new OnePlus 5... hope it brings you joy" — a full sentiment inversion, scoring the worst possible 1.0/1.0 on groundedness and hallucination from the LLM judge. Pure lexical/semantic similarity retrieval has no polarity check between the incoming complaint and the retrieved candidate's sentiment.
4. **Near-verbatim copying persists at real magnitude despite an earlier fix** (a known, inherent RAG tradeoff, not fully fixable without a quality cost). A price-match question got a reply with 92% token overlap with its top-1 retrieved example — the model essentially recited history rather than drafting. Tightening the no-copy instruction further risks the opposite failure (drifting from the grounded example, reintroducing hallucination risk).
5. **The name-ban safety heuristic false-positives on capitalized common nouns** (fixable, low priority). "Diwali" (a festival) tripped the name-leak detector meant to catch leaked customer/agent names. Trivial allowlist fix, functionally harmless today since the flag is informational only.

## 4. What's misleading about my headline number

Several numbers in this report look better or worse than they should in isolation:

- **Escalation accuracy alone hides the precision/recall tradeoff entirely.** The full system's escalation accuracy (0.525) looks mediocre next to the simple baseline's 0.670 — but that comparison is backwards. The simple baseline's accuracy is inflated by almost never predicting `escalate` at all (recall 0.043) against a golden set where 65.5% of rows are true-auto; a model that just says "auto" nearly every time gets rewarded by accuracy while missing 96% of the escalations that matter. Precision and recall separately, not accuracy alone, are the numbers that tell the truth here.
- **The simple baseline's 0.670 escalation accuracy is near-tautological.** It is barely above the golden set's true-auto base rate (131/200 = 65.5%), which is what a model predicting `auto` for everything would score. It is not evidence the model understands escalation — it's evidence the label distribution is imbalanced and accuracy is the wrong metric to lead with.
- **The judge shares a model with the generator (Phi-3-mini) — a real, not theoretical, self-preference-bias risk.** No second local model was available within budget/hardware after the Gemini pivot. The judge prompt is deliberately reworded and reframed (skeptical external auditor vs. brand-voice assistant) and decoded differently (temperature=0.3 sampling vs. the generator's greedy decoding), but this is mitigation, not elimination. Concrete evidence it's not just theoretical: the `tone_match` dimension — plausibly the one most sensitive to a model favoring its own generation style — is exactly the dimension where calibration against human scores failed (Spearman ρ=-0.079, p=0.630, not significant) despite the *highest* raw exact-or-adjacent agreement (87.5%) of any dimension. The other four dimensions (relevance ρ=0.407 p=0.009, groundedness ρ=0.375 p=0.017, no_hallucinated_specifics ρ=0.397 p=0.011, actionability ρ=0.451 p=0.003, all n=40) correlate significantly with human judgment; tone_match — the most self-referential dimension — does not. That is not proof of self-preference bias specifically, but it is exactly the pattern that bias would produce, and it's a real measured result, not a caveat pulled from a methods textbook.
- **The golden set is deliberately non-representative — any full-200 metric over-weights hard cases.** 40 of the 200 rows (20%) are oversampled stress tests (fraud keywords, near-verbatim retrieval stress, pay/paid-for vocabulary overlap, low retrieval similarity), which do not occur at anywhere near 20% frequency in real traffic. Every headline number in Section 2 is computed on the full 200 and will therefore read somewhat worse than real-world performance; an unbiased stratified-only number (n=160, the non-hard-case subset) is available in the underlying data (`hard_case_category` column) for anyone who wants the more representative figure instead.
- **The predicted-intent distribution skewed harder than expected during golden-set construction.** The 160-row stratified sample was drawn using the *fast baseline classifier's* predictions to balance across all 8 intents (roughly 20-33 rows per intent). When the actual LLM classifier ran over the same 200 rows, its predictions concentrated much more heavily on `general_complaint_other` (65) and `delivery_shipping_issue` (48) — 56.5% of the 200 combined. That gap between the cheap stratification proxy and the real classifier's actual behavior is itself informative: the baseline and the full system disagree substantially on what a given tweet even is, before touching accuracy against ground truth at all.

## 5. Next-week plan

**Highest leverage first: fix the refund/return vs. delivery/billing vocabulary-overlap confusion (failure modes #1 and #2).** This is the single fix that does double duty — it's both the dominant misclassification pattern in the golden set and the direct cause of the majority of missed escalations, the most safety-relevant error category in the whole system. Concretely: add few-shot examples to the classifier prompt that explicitly contrast "customer wants money back" against "customer mentions a delivery or payment method in passing," mirroring the fix already proven effective for the earlier billing-vs-delivery service-level confusion. Re-run against the golden set to confirm the missed-escalation count drops below 9 before touching anything else.

After that, in priority order:
- Investigate confidence miscalibration directly (every refund/return misclassification was at 0.95 confidence) — consider a calibration pass or a second, disagreement-based confidence signal rather than trusting the model's self-reported number at face value.
- Add a lightweight sentiment/polarity check between the incoming tweet and retrieved RAG candidates before drafting, to catch failure mode #3 (grounding tone as well as content) without a second model.
- Get a second model into the judge role — even a small distinct model would let the self-preference-bias risk in Section 4 be tested empirically instead of just documented.
- Expand the `COMMON_WORDS` allowlist in `draft_reply.py`'s name-leak check (failure mode #5) — low effort, removes a source of noisy validation warnings.
- Revisit the escalation precision tradeoff once the intent-classifier fix lands: if fixing #1/#2 meaningfully improves the classifier's reliability on the high-risk intents, it may become safe to loosen the always-escalate rule for the highest-confidence cases within those intents, recovering some of the 86 over-escalations without reopening the recall gap.
